## Pick the right mix for the job

Concrete isn't one thing — it's a family of mixes engineered for different loads and conditions. The mix you use depends on the job:

- **C8 / C10** — non-structural fill, blinding, kerb backing. Lean mix.
- **C20 / C25** — the everyday mix for shed bases, patios, fence posts, foundations for garden walls.
- **C30 / C35** — structural slabs, garage floors, driveways that take car weight, ring beams.
- **C40 and above** — commercial floors, structural beams, agricultural yards.

If you're not sure, C20 is the right default for domestic DIY. Buy a **quality-assured bagged concrete** (post-mix, foundation concrete, or "C20" pre-blend) and you'll get a consistent mix every batch — no measuring, no wondering if your ratios are correct.

> **Tip:** DIYers most often ruin concrete by using a sand-and-cement mortar mix (1:3) instead of a proper concrete mix (which has aggregate — gravel or ballast — for strength). A 1:3 sand-cement mix is mortar; it has no large aggregate and cracks under anything heavier than foot traffic.

## Calculate how much you need

Volume = length × width × depth, in metres. Then add **10%** for spillage, uneven ground and over-excavation. Always round up to the nearest 50kg bag or 0.25m³ if you're ordering ready-mix.

| Job | Typical size | Concrete needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fence post hole (75mm round × 600mm deep) | n posts | ~0.04m³ each |
| Standard garden shed base (3m × 3m × 100mm) | — | ~1.0m³ |
| Patio slab (under the slabs, not the bedding sand) | per m² at 100mm thick | 0.1m³ per m² |
| Driveway (4m × 6m × 150mm) | — | ~4.0m³ |

For larger pours (anything over about 1m³), it's almost always cheaper and faster to order **ready-mix concrete by the metre** than to mix it yourself.

## Mix by hand — for small jobs

Hand-mixing works for batches up to about 100kg total. You need a smooth, flat surface — a mixing board, a concrete tray, or the back of a wheelbarrow with the handles braced.

1. **Measure your materials** by bucket — one bucket of cement, two of **sharp sand**, four of **20mm ballast** for a C20 mix.
2. **Combine dry** — empty the aggregate onto the board first, then the sand, then the cement on top. Turn it over with a spade until the colour is uniform. This is the only way to get an even distribution of cement before you add any water.
3. **Form a crater** in the middle of the pile. Add about half a bucket of clean water to start.
4. **Shovel from the outside in**, mixing as you go, adding small amounts of water until the concrete is the consistency of thick porridge. It should hold its shape on the spade without slumping; if it slumps back, it's too wet.
5. **Use it within an hour.** Concrete starts setting as soon as water hits the cement.

## Mix with a small mixer — for medium jobs

Hire a 90-litre or 120-litre cement mixer for about £30 a day. They're worth the money for anything over 100kg of concrete. Two people can mix and place about 1m³ in a long afternoon if the access and the weather cooperate.

1. **Tip the aggregate in first**, then the sand, then the cement.
2. Run the mixer for a minute to dry-mix.
3. **Add water gradually** through the hopper — start with about half a bucket, wait 30 seconds, add more until the mix comes together. Most operators add too much water; the mix should be just-wet, not sloshing.
4. **Tip the mixer** into a wheelbarrow while it's still turning to keep the mix tumbling. Don't let it slump and stop — that means it's starting to set.
5. **Wash out the drum** at the end of the job — hardened concrete is a nightmare to remove once cured.

> **Common mistake:** leaving the mixer running between batches. It's tempting, but the mix sets against the drum and reduces capacity batch after batch. Stop it; tip it; restart.

## Place, compact and cure

Concrete starts to set within 30–60 minutes of mixing, so get it into place quickly:

1. **Tip into the formwork / hole / trench.** Don't drop it from more than about 1m — it will segregate (the aggregate falls out and the cement-rich slurry rises).
2. **Compact** with a vibrating poker for deep pours, or by tamping and tapping the formwork with a hammer for shallow slabs. Compaction pushes air bubbles out and gets the aggregate down into the cement paste — without it the surface will be honeycombed and weak.
3. **Level and tamp** the surface with a timber straight-edge (a "screed board") worked back and forth across the top of the formwork. Then float it smooth with a magnesium float, then steel-trowel for a hard finish if required.
4. **Cure for at least 3 days** — and ideally 7. Cover with polythene, damp hessian, or spray with a curing compound. Concrete cures by hydration, so it needs moisture, not heat. Letting it dry out is the most common cause of dusty, cracked surfaces.

## Materials and tools checklist

**Materials**

- Portland cement (or bagged C20 pre-mix)
- Sharp sand
- 20mm (or 10mm) coarse aggregate / all-in-ballast
- Clean water
- Plasticiser (optional — improves workability)

**Tools**

- Wheelbarrow or mixing board (hand-mixing)
- Cement mixer (hire for medium jobs)
- Spade / shovel
- Bucket (for measuring)
- Vibrating poker (for deep pours)
- Straight-edge for screeding
- Magnesium float and steel trowel
- Polythene sheeting for curing

## When to order ready-mix or hire a specialist

For anything over 1m³, the case for ready-mix gets strong — cheaper materials, consistent mix, no mixer to wash out, and a one-shot pour means no cold joints. Call in a specialist if:

- The pour is structural (foundations, beams, garage floors)
- The volume is over 3m³ (you'll spend the entire day mixing by hand)
- Site access is awkward or you need a concrete pump
- You need a specified mix (e.g. C35 with fibre reinforcement for a driveway)
