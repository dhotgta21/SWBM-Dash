## Plan the route and check the rules

Before you start, decide where the tap will go (out of the way of foot traffic, ideally 300mm above ground for clearance) and how the pipework will run from inside the house to outside.

UK Water Regulations require any outdoor tap to have:

- An **isolation valve** inside the house, on the cold feed.
- A **single check valve** to stop water in the garden hose siphoning back into your drinking supply.
- A **frost-protected tap body** with drain-down capability, or you isolate the supply in winter.

Most builders' merchant-grade outdoor tap kits contain the tap, the through-wall fitting, an isolation valve and the check valve. Get a **WRAS-approved** kit — it's the standard the water authorities accept.

> **Tip:** tee off the **kitchen cold** (it's the closest pipe in most houses and easy to isolate). Run the pipe in 15mm copper or 15mm plastic barrier pipe — never push-fit inside a wall without a protective sleeve.

## Drill the hole

Pick a position on the outside wall about 300–500mm above ground level (so a watering can fits under it without stooping, but the tap is high enough to clear a bucket). Avoid drilling through the visible face of a feature wall or rendered finish if you can — go through a less visible side.

1. **Check the wall** with a multi-detector for cables and pipework. Stop and seek advice if you find live cables.
2. **Mark the hole position** on the outside wall, and on the inside wall in line with it. Use a spirit level to line the two up.
3. **Drill from inside the house** through to outside using a 22mm diamond-tipped masonry drill bit for a 15mm pipe. Drill slowly, withdrawing the bit every 5 seconds to clear dust and cool the diamond. Stop just as you break through to the outside face — you don't want a "blow-out" patch on the visible brickwork.

If you hit a cavity, the bit will drop through into the gap — drill through the outer leaf as well from the outside, again using the hole as a guide.

> **Common mistake:** drilling through the **DPC** (damp-proof course). The DPC is normally a few bricks above ground, just above the floor level. Cut a small channel in the plaster to redirect the pipe slightly if you need to dip below the DPC, then seal the pipe entry with silicone so water can't track along the outside of the pipe into the wall.

## Tee into the cold water supply

Turn off the cold water at the main stopcock and drain the pipework by opening the kitchen tap.

Cut the cold water pipe at the tee point (a short stub of exposed pipe, often under the kitchen sink or in the cupboard) using a **pipe cutter** (not a hacksaw — hacksaw cuts leave burrs). Fit a **15mm compression tee** to the pipe, then run **15mm pipe** to the hole through the wall, securing it with pipe clips every 600mm.

At the inside end of the hole, fit an **isolation valve** with the arrow pointing in the direction of flow. Then a **double-check valve** (or, in most kits, a combined isolation + check valve fitting). This is your one job to do well — if the check valve fails the water authority will require the whole fitting to be ripped out.

## Fit the through-wall fitting and the outside pipe

Push the pipe through the wall to the outside. From the outside, fit the **through-wall plate** — a brass flange with a 15mm compression outlet that screws to the wall and seals against the brickwork with a rubber gasket. Use **stainless-steel screws and wall plugs** so the plate doesn't rust off in two winters.

Cut a short stub of 15mm pipe (about 100mm) to go up from the through-wall plate to the tap inlet, then **fit the tap**. Hand-tighten the tap, then nip up with a spanner — over-tightening will crack the brass body.

## Turn it on and test

Open the isolation valve slowly and check every joint for leaks with a dry tissue — even a slow drip is worth tightening now. Run the tap for a minute to flush any debris out of the new pipework, then close it and check again at the joints.

> **Tip:** if a joint drips, don't keep tightening — that risks cracking the olive. Instead, slacken off, check the olive is correctly seated, and re-tighten.

Wrap the body of the tap with **insulation** (foam pipe lagging or a purpose-made tap cover) before the first frost. Even a frost-protected tap will benefit from insulation — it takes the edge off the worst weather.

## Materials and tools checklist

**Materials**

- Frost-protected outdoor tap with integral check valve
- Through-wall plate and gaskets
- 15mm compression tee
- Isolation valve (often included in tap kit)
- 15mm copper or plastic barrier pipe
- Pipe clips and wall plugs
- Pipe insulation / foam tap cover
- Silicone sealant (for the through-wall entry)
- PTFE tape

**Tools**

- Pipe cutter
- 22mm diamond-tipped masonry drill bit (and SDS or hammer drill)
- Multi-detector
- Adjustable spanners and pipe wrench
- Spirit level
- PTFE tape

## When to call a plumber

In many UK water authorities, work on the **consumer's pipework** (the pipework inside the property boundary up to the meter) is something you can do yourself. If you're not confident with compression fittings or pushing a pipe through the wall cleanly, the job is a quick one for a plumber — typically 1–2 hours and well worth it. Call a plumber if:

- You're unsure how to tee into the existing pipework
- The route involves running through floorboards or hidden voids
- The wall is structural, multi-leaf or has hidden utilities
- You've accidentally drilled into a cable or pipework (turn off electricity / water and call an emergency plumber)
