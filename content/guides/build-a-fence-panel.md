## Plan the run

Measure the length of the fence and divide it into **panel sections** — 1.8m or 2.0m is the standard spacing for most timber fence panels. Mark the position of each post. Check for services (gas, water, electric, BT) with a **CAT scanner** before you dig — fines for hitting a cable run into four figures.

Most domestic fences can be up to **2m tall** without planning permission (1m if the fence fronts a highway). Side-by-side, the higher of the two fences owns the rule, so if your neighbour has a 1.8m fence, you can match it without permission. Conservation areas, listed buildings and new-builds often have stricter rules.

> **Tip:** before you dig, run a string line between the start and end of the fence run to confirm it's straight and on your side of any boundary. Hedges, fences and walls often move over decades; the legal boundary is often somewhere you don't expect.

## Dig the post holes

Each post hole should be **about 600mm deep** for an 1.8m fence, more in soft ground. As a rule of thumb, **a third of the post length should be in the ground**. The width is roughly three times the post dimension — about 250mm × 250mm for a 75mm post.

Tip **100mm of gravel** into the bottom of each hole for drainage — without it, water pools around the foot of the post and rots it from below. Place the post on a couple of bricks in the gravel to hold it upright, then **brace it plumb in both directions** using two timber battens screwed to the post and staked into the ground.

> **Common mistake:** setting the post too shallow in soft ground. In clay soils especially, frost action lifts the post year on year — within a season you'll see the post lean and the fence lean with it. Go at least 600mm deep, more in clay.

## Set the posts in post-mix

**Post-mix concrete** is the right way to fix fence posts — it sets in minutes and you don't need to mix anything. Fill the hole to within 50–100mm of the surface with the dry (or pre-dampened, depending on the brand) post-mix and water. Most post-mixes cure fully within 10 minutes — by the time you've moved on to the next hole, the previous post is set.

Re-check the plumb after the post-mix goes off — if it has moved, you have about 30 seconds to nudge it back before it sets hard.

Use a **string line** between the first and last post to line up all the intermediate posts. Check the height with a spirit level or laser.

## Fit the rails

Cut the **arris rails** (the horizontal timbers that hold the featheredge boards) to span between adjacent posts. Use **rail brackets** or **arris rail mortice joints** cut into the post to fix them at the right heights: typically a bottom rail about 150mm above the gravel board, a top rail about 150mm below the top of the post, and one or two intermediate rails between.

Most UK fence installations use **three rails** for a 1.8m fence: bottom, middle, top. The boards are then fixed to the rails with the featheredge "feather" running from low to high so each board overlaps the one below by 25mm or so.

Check each rail is level before fixing — a wonky rail means a wonky fence line.

## Fix the featheredge boards

**Featheredge boards** are tapered timber boards (usually 125mm × 22mm at the thick end, tapering to 3mm or so at the thin end). They overlap to form the "close-boarded" face. Fix them with **galvanised or stainless-steel nails**, two per rail.

Start at the bottom of the fence with the thick end down (so water runs off the rounded face), then fix each subsequent board so it overlaps the lower board by about 25mm. Use a **board spacer** or offcut to set the overlap — keep it consistent or the fence looks ragged.

> **Tip:** stagger the joints between boards so you don't get a vertical seam every metre. Pick boards that fit the run and cut to length so the joints don't land on the same rail.

## Add the gravel board and capping

The **gravel board** is a pressure-treated timber board (or a pre-cast concrete board) that sits on the ground under the rails, lifting the featheredge boards off the wet ground. Slot it into **gravel board brackets** fixed to each post. **Concrete gravel boards** last 25+ years; timber ones will need replacing within 10–15.

Cap the top of each post with a **post cap** (a small pressure-treated or decorative timber cap) to stop water sitting on the cut end of the post and rotting it down.

## Materials and tools checklist

**Materials**

- Fence posts (75mm × 75mm × 1.8m, UC4-treated for ground contact)
- Arris rails (typically 75mm × 75mm × 1.8m)
- Featheredge boards (typically 125mm × 22mm, sold in bundles of 5)
- Gravel board (timber or concrete)
- Post-mix concrete
- Rail brackets and gravel board brackets
- Stainless-steel or galvanised nails
- Post caps
- Timber battens (for bracing)

**Tools**

- Tape measure and string line
- Post-hole digger or spade
- Spirit level (long, or a laser)
- Hand saw or circular saw
- Hammer or nail gun
- CAT scanner (for buried services)

## When to call a fencer

Six fence panels in a straight run is a perfectly achievable weekend; a multi-level fence with returns, gates, or built-in planters is more involved. Get a fencer in if:

- The fence is over 2m tall (planning permission + safety)
- You're replacing a boundary fence with a different material (legal position may need a party-wall agreement)
- The ground is heavy clay or very stony
- The run is long (over about 20m) and needs a concrete or gravel-board base
- You want matching posts and panels for an existing run
