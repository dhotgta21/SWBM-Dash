## Check what you've got

UK Building Regulations require **270mm of insulation** at ceiling level. That depth has been the standard since 2021 and gives a U-value of about 0.16 W/m²K — enough to keep the heat inside and the cold out for most domestic properties.

Before you start, work out what's there:

- A loft built before the late 1970s likely has **100mm or no insulation**.
- A loft insulated between 2000 and 2020 probably has **200–250mm**.
- Anything at or above 270mm already is up to current standards.

If you have downlighters in the ceiling below, make sure they're **rated "IC" or "F" for use with insulation** — older fire-rated downlighters can overheat when buried in insulation, creating a real fire risk. Replace any that aren't rated, or use **downlighter covers** to keep the insulation off them.

> **Tip:** check the loft hatch first — old latches pull cold air in from the loft. A draught-proofed, insulated loft hatch is one of the cheapest energy-saving improvements you can make.

## Clear and prep the loft

Most lofts have years of accumulated stuff. Be ruthless — toss anything you don't want, move everything you do want into one corner (boxed and stacked neatly), and **board out the central area** if you want to keep using the loft for light storage. Loft boards are sold in packs of 3 or 4 m², and they slot onto the joists butting up against the cross-laid insulation on top.

**Sweep the loft clean** before you start. Check for damp patches on the felt, missing tiles on the roof, daylight showing through — fix any of those before you insulate, or you'll bury a problem that gets worse.

> **Common mistake:** burying existing pipework and electric cable in insulation. Identify anything that needs to be reachable — water tanks, central heating headers, electric junction boxes — and **build a raised platform over them** so you can still access them after the insulation is down.

## Wear the right gear

Mineral wool itch. **Wear a long-sleeved top, long trousers, gloves and a face mask** (FFP2 or better) before you climb into the loft. Eye protection is also strongly recommended — stuff falling from above is one of the top DIY injury causes.

Have a helper at the loft hatch handing you rolls, tools and a clipboard. You don't want to climb back down every five minutes, and you really don't want to step wrong and put your foot through the ceiling below.

## Lay the first layer between the joists

Standard ceiling joists are at **400mm centres**, so 400mm-wide insulation rolls fit between them perfectly. Start at the eaves and work backwards towards the loft hatch.

**Roll out** the first layer with the paper or foil vapour-check side facing **down** (towards the room below), so the vapour check stops moisture from the house rising into the insulation. Butt the edges tightly together — gaps between rolls halve the insulation performance along that gap.

Cut the rolls to length with a **long-blade knife or old bread knife**, compressing the wool slightly as you cut so the next piece springs back into place. Pack the cut-off into the eaves and odd gaps so there's no un-insulated roof.

> **Tip:** don't compress insulation to fit. Compressed wool loses 50% of its performance. If the joist spacing is awkward, buy narrower or wider rolls to suit, not smaller compression.

## Cross-lay the second layer over the joists

Once the whole loft has a layer between the joists, **lay a second layer at right angles** to the first — running across the joists themselves. This second layer covers the timber joists (which are otherwise a thermal bridge) and takes the total depth up to 270mm.

Most builders use a thinner roll for the second layer — typically **170mm rolls for the first layer, 100mm for the second**, both laid to give 270mm total. Butt the edges tightly together and run them wall-to-wall, leaving 50mm clearance around any flue or heat-source.

If you want to **board out the loft for storage**, the cross-laid second layer is what you board directly onto — fit the boards in a staggered pattern, screwing down through the boards into the joists every 600mm.

## Seal the loft hatch and the eaves

Two easy wins often get missed:

- **Loft hatch.** Make an insulated lid — glue a 50mm piece of rigid insulation board to the back of the existing loft hatch, and fit draught-excluder tape around the rim. This stops the hatch acting as a 600mm × 600mm cooling fin all winter.
- **Eaves.** Push insulation between the last joist and the wall, right into the eaves. **Don't block the soffit ventilation** — leave a clear 25mm air gap between the top of the insulation and the underside of the roof felt. Use **eaves ventilators** if you need to keep the insulation higher and still vent the loft.

Insulate any **pipework in the loft** — water pipes especially. Cold-water pipes will freeze the first frost they pass through an unheated loft, and lag is much cheaper than a burst pipe.

## Materials and tools checklist

**Materials**

- 170mm mineral wool rolls for the first layer (between joists)
- 100mm mineral wool rolls for the second layer (cross-laid over joists)
- Loft-legs or battens (if boarding for storage)
- Loft boards (optional, for storage)
- Rigid insulation board for the loft hatch
- Draught-excluder tape for the hatch rim
- Pipe insulation (for any pipework in the loft)
- Downlighter covers (if needed)

**Tools**

- Long-sleeved top, gloves, FFP2 mask, eye protection
- Long-blade knife or bread knife for cutting wool
- Tape measure
- Staple gun (to fix the vapour check to the joists if needed)

## When to call a specialist

A simple rectangular loft in a terraced or detached house is a confident DIY project; an awkward loft with dormers, multiple heights, or pre-existing spray foam is more involved. Get a specialist in if:

- The loft has spray foam insulation that needs removing or re-doing
- The roof structure has a history of condensation or rot
- You want to upgrade to a "warm-roof" system as well as insulating the ceiling
- The joists aren't deep enough to take the target depth
- The ceiling has many downlighters that need upgrading or covers
