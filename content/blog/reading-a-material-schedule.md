When you receive a builder's quote, the price is only part of the story. What really matters is what you get for that price. A material schedule, also called a bill of materials or specification, lists every product the builder intends to use. Understanding how to read it protects you from poor substitutions, hidden shortfalls and misunderstandings.

## What is a material schedule?

A material schedule is a document that sets out the products required for a building project. It usually includes:

- Product names and descriptions.
- Manufacturers and brand names.
- Product codes or model numbers.
- Quantities and units.
- Sizes and dimensions.
- Quality standards or British Standards references.
- Where each product is used in the build.

A good schedule leaves no room for doubt. Instead of "insulation," it says "100 mm rigid PIR insulation to BS EN 13165." Instead of "tiles," it gives the manufacturer, colour, size and slip rating. You can check British Standards through the [British Standards Institution](https://www.bsigroup.com/) or ask your merchant for guidance.

## Why the schedule matters

Two builders can quote very different prices for the same project because they are specifying very different materials. A cheap quote may use lower-grade products, thinner insulation, standard windows instead of energy-efficient ones, or unbranded fixings.

Reading the schedule lets you compare like for like. It also gives you a basis for discussion if you want to upgrade, downgrade or substitute a product.

## The main sections of a material schedule

Material schedules vary, but most cover the same building elements.

### Foundations and groundworks

This section lists concrete, reinforcement, damp-proof membranes, drainage pipes, manholes and backfill materials. Check the concrete strength, reinforcement specification and whether the damp-proof course is included.

### Structural materials

This includes timber, steel beams, lintels, wall ties, mortar and blocks or bricks. Look for grade marks, treatment specifications and British Standards. For example, structural timber should show a strength class such as C16 or C24.

### Insulation

Check the type, thickness and thermal performance. Products should reference current building regulation requirements. Thin or low-performance insulation can cause compliance problems and higher energy bills.

### Windows and doors

A schedule should list the manufacturer, frame material, glazing specification, U-values and hardware finishes. "PVC windows" is not enough. "White uPVC casement window, A-rated, double glazed, argon filled" is better.

### Roofing

This includes tiles or slates, battens, felt or membrane, leadwork, gutters and downpipes. Check compatibility with existing roof coverings and whether the builder has allowed for waste.

### Cladding and finishes

Brick, render, timber cladding, fascias and soffits should all be specified with manufacturer and colour where relevant. Vague terms like "matching render" can lead to disputes.

### Electrical and plumbing

A material schedule for first and second fix should list cable sizes, consumer unit specification, sockets, switches, pipework, fittings and sanitaryware. Electrical work must comply with Part P of the building regulations.

### Fixings, adhesives and sealants

Small items matter. The wrong screws, adhesive or sealant can cause failures that are expensive to put right. A thorough schedule lists these even if they seem minor.

## Provisional sums and prime cost sums

Schedules sometimes include provisional sums or prime cost sums rather than fixed prices. A provisional sum is an allowance for work whose full extent is not yet known. A prime cost sum is an allowance for the supply of items that have not been fully specified, such as sanitaryware or light fittings.

These allowances are not final prices. If the actual cost is higher, you pay the difference. If it is lower, you should receive a credit. Make sure you understand which items are provisional and ask your builder to update the figures as soon as choices are made.

## How to check quantities

Quantity is where many schedules fall short. Builders typically add a percentage for waste, but the allowance should be reasonable. Too little and the project stalls while more materials are ordered. Too much and you are paying for products you do not use.

Check that quantities match the drawings and specification. If the schedule lists 150 square metres of floor tiles but the floor area is 120 square metres, ask what the extra is for. It may be a sensible cutting and wastage allowance, or it may be an error.

## Understanding substitutions

Schedules often include phrases like "or equal approved" or "or similar." These mean the builder can propose an alternative, but only if it is genuinely equivalent. You should approve any substitution in writing.

Be wary of vague phrases like "builder's standard" or "as selected." These give the builder too much discretion. Ask for specific products or a range to choose from.

## Comparing schedules between builders

When comparing quotes, lay the schedules side by side. Look for differences in:

- Brand and product quality.
- Thickness and performance of insulation.
- Window and door U-values.
- Structural timber grades.
- Roofing material quality.
- Waste allowances.

A higher quote is often justified by better materials. A lower quote may rely on cutting corners. Our guide to [how to compare builder quotes](/blog/how-to-compare-builder-quotes) explains the full process.

## Who supplies the materials?

Some builders include materials in their price. Others quote for labour only and expect you to supply materials. Each approach has pros and cons.

If the builder supplies materials, they are responsible for quality, delivery and waste. If you supply materials, you may save money but you take on coordination risk. ## Ask for samples and data sheets

For key finishes such as tiles, flooring, worktops, doors and ironmongery, ask to see samples before work starts. For technical products such as insulation, windows and membranes, ask for manufacturer's data sheets. These confirm performance claims and help your building control officer approve the work.

## What to do if the schedule is unclear

If a schedule is vague, ask the builder to clarify before you sign a contract. Questions to ask include:

- What brand and model is this product?
- What British Standard or equivalent does it meet?
- What happens if that product is unavailable?
- Is delivery, unloading and storage included?
- What allowance has been made for waste?
- Are there any provisional sums or estimated quantities?

A professional builder will welcome these questions. A reluctant or evasive builder is a warning sign.

## Building regulations and warranties

The materials used must comply with building regulations. Using substandard products can cause building control to reject work and may invalidate warranties. Keep copies of receipts, data sheets and certificates for products such as windows, boilers, insulation and membranes. For more information, see [GOV.UK building regulations approval](https://www.gov.uk/building-regulations-approval).

## Final thoughts

A material schedule is one of the most useful documents in a building project. It turns a vague quote into a clear plan and gives you a way to compare builders fairly. Take time to read it, ask questions and agree any substitutions in writing.
