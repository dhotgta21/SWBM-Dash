Every building project involves a tension between cost and quality. Homeowners naturally want to keep spending under control, but choosing the cheapest option for every material is one of the fastest ways to create problems later. Leaks, cold spots, structural movement and premature failure are expensive to put right.

The trick is knowing where quality matters most and where a cheaper product will do the same job. This guide explains how to make those decisions for a typical UK extension, renovation or self-build.

## Why the cheapest quote is not always the cheapest project

A builder can lower a quote by using thinner insulation, lower-grade timber, cheaper fixings or unbranded membranes. The finished project may look the same on day one, but the difference shows up over time:

- Cold bridging and higher energy bills.
- Roof leaks or condensation problems.
- Cracking movement in walls.
- Rot or decay in poorly treated timber.
- Failed fixings and squeaky floors.

Spending a little more on the right materials upfront usually saves money, disruption and regret.

## Where to prioritise quality

Some parts of a building are hard or impossible to upgrade later. These are the areas where quality should win over price.

### Foundations and groundworks

You cannot see foundations after the build, but everything rests on them. Use the correct concrete mix, reinforcement and damp-proof membrane for your soil conditions. Skimping here risks settlement cracks and damp that are disruptive and costly to repair.

Always confirm that concrete and reinforcement meet the specification in your structural engineer's calculations. Any substitution should be agreed in writing.

### Structural timber

Carcassing timber should meet the correct strength grade for the span and load. C16 is common for general studwork and joists. C24 is stronger and better for longer spans or heavier loads. Buying lower-grade timber to save money can cause deflection, bounce and fixing problems.

Timber should also be properly seasoned and stored. Wet or twisted timber will shrink, warp and split after installation.

### Structural steel

Steel beams, lintels and fixings must be specified by a structural engineer. Using undersized steel or the wrong bolts is dangerous and will fail building control. You can read more about structural safety standards on the [NHBC website](https://www.nhbc.co.uk/).

### Damp-proofing and weatherproofing

A good damp-proof course, cavity trays, breathable membranes and quality flashing are essential. A single failure here can cause damp patches, mould and timber decay that cost thousands to repair.

### Insulation

Insulation affects comfort, energy bills and compliance. Cheaper insulation may have a lower thermal conductivity, meaning you need more thickness to hit the same U-value. Poor installation creates cold bridges. Pay attention to the product's lambda value and how easily it fits the cavity or frame.

For most extensions, insulation must meet the standards set out in [building regulations approval](https://www.gov.uk/building-regulations-approval). Your builder or merchant will know which products comply for your application.

### Roofing

The roof protects everything beneath it. Quality tiles, slates, battens, membrane and fascia boards last decades. Cheap roofing felt or poor-quality tiles can fail early and let water in.

### Windows and doors

Better glazing, frames and seals reduce heat loss, noise and maintenance. A cheap window may need replacing in ten years. A quality window can last thirty. Look for energy ratings, guarantees and proper installation details.

## Where you can sensibly save money

Not every material needs to be top of the range. There are places where a mid-range product performs just as well as a premium one.

### Decorative finishes

Paint, wallpaper, tiles and floor coverings are easy to upgrade later. A mid-range tile from a reputable brand is usually fine. You do not need handmade tiles unless the look is essential to the design.

### Internal doors and ironmongery

Standard internal doors are functional and affordable. Save the budget for external doors, where security and weatherproofing matter more.

### Standard fixings and site consumables

Nails, screws, wall plugs and adhesives should be good quality, but you rarely need the most expensive brand. Buy from a reputable merchant and avoid unbranded bulk packs of unknown origin.

### Standard plasterboard

For most internal walls and ceilings, standard 12.5mm plasterboard is perfectly adequate. Upgrade to moisture-resistant, fire-resistant or acoustic boards only where the location demands it.

## How to compare materials fairly

When choosing between two products, look beyond the price per unit. Consider:

- **Specification.** Does it meet the relevant British Standard or building regulation requirement?
- **Coverage.** A cheaper product may cover less area, so the total cost can be higher.
- **Durability.** How long is it expected to last?
- **Warranty.** Does the manufacturer offer a meaningful guarantee?
- **Compatibility.** Will it work with the other materials in the build?
- **Availability.** A bargain product that is out of stock for six weeks can delay the project.

Ask your builder or merchant for a side-by-side comparison. A good supplier will explain the difference honestly.

## The hidden cost of poor specification

Using the wrong material can be more expensive than using a better one. Examples include:

- Cheap mortar that crumbles and needs repointing.
- Lightweight blocks that cannot support the load above them.
- Non-breathable membranes that trap moisture in old walls.
- Incompatible adhesives that fail under heat or damp.

Your builder and merchant are your best defence against these mistakes. If a price looks unusually low, ask what product is being quoted and why.

## Value engineering without cutting corners

Value engineering means getting the same result for less money, not getting a worse result for less money. Ways to do this include:

- Simplifying the design to reduce waste and labour.
- Choosing locally available materials to cut delivery costs.
- Buying in bulk where storage allows.
- Reusing existing materials where appropriate, such as bricks or roof tiles.
- Timing orders to take advantage of trade promotions.

## Should you let your builder choose materials?

For structural and trade materials, usually yes. A good builder knows which products perform well, which are readily available and which ones their team is used to working with. They also take responsibility if those materials fail.

For decorative items, you may prefer to choose yourself. Just make sure you confirm specifications, delivery dates and compatibility with your builder. For more on this, see our guide to [whether you should buy materials or let your builder](/blog/should-you-buy-materials-or-let-builder).

## The role of certification and standards

Look for materials that carry relevant certification:

- **CE or UKCA marking** on structural products.
- **British Standards** such as BS EN 771 for bricks or BS 4978 for timber.
- **BBA certificates** for membranes, insulation and systems.
- **FSC or PEFC** certification for sustainably sourced timber.

These marks mean the product has been tested and meets minimum standards. Unbranded or uncertified materials are a gamble.

Reputable trade bodies such as the [Federation of Master Builders](https://www.fmb.org.uk/find-a-builder) expect their members to use appropriate, compliant materials. Choosing a builder who belongs to a recognised scheme is another layer of protection.

## Final thoughts

Choosing building materials is not about finding the cheapest price or the most premium brand. It is about matching the right product to the right job. Invest in the parts of the building that are hard to change and protect the structure from weather, damp and movement. Save on the decorative items that can be updated later.
