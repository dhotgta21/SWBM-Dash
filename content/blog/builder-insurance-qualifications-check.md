Hiring a builder is one of the biggest financial decisions a homeowner makes. Price and availability matter, but nothing matters more than knowing the person you hire is competent, insured and honest. A few simple checks at the start can save you from cowboy builders, unsafe work and expensive disputes later.

This guide explains what insurance and qualifications to look for, how to verify them and what to do if something does not add up.

## Why insurance matters

Building work involves risk. Heavy materials, power tools, excavations, heights and structural alterations can all cause injury or damage. If something goes wrong and your builder is not properly insured, you could be left paying the bill.

Insurance is also a sign of professionalism. A builder who takes the trouble to arrange proper cover is more likely to take the rest of their business seriously.

## Public liability insurance

Public liability insurance covers claims if the builder causes injury to someone other than their own employees, or damage to your property or a neighbour's property. It is the minimum cover any builder should have.

Typical cover limits range from £1 million to £5 million. For larger projects or work near third-party property, higher limits are sensible. Ask to see the certificate and check the expiry date.

## Employers' liability insurance

If the builder has employees, labourers or apprentices, employers' liability insurance is a legal requirement in almost all cases. It covers claims from staff injured while working for the builder.

Ask whether anyone working on your project is employed by the builder or subcontracted. Subcontractors should have their own insurance, and you should ask for proof.

## Contractors all-risk insurance

Contractors all-risk insurance covers the works themselves while they are under construction. This can include damage from fire, storm, theft of materials and accidental damage.

For major renovations or extensions, make sure the contract or builder's insurance covers the works in progress. If your own home insurance excludes building work, this cover becomes even more important.

## Professional indemnity insurance

If your builder is providing design services, structural advice or drawings, professional indemnity insurance protects you if their design is faulty. This is particularly relevant for design-and-build contracts where the builder takes on the role of designer as well as contractor.

## Warranty and guarantee insurance

Some builders offer insurance-backed warranties through providers such as [NHBC](https://www.nhbc.co.uk/) or similar schemes. These warranties can cover defects that appear after completion and may be transferable if you sell the property.

Check what the warranty covers, how long it lasts and whether there are any exclusions. An insurance-backed warranty is more valuable than a builder's verbal promise to come back and fix things.

## How to verify insurance

Do not just take a builder's word for it. Follow these steps:

1. Ask for a current certificate of insurance.
2. Check the policyholder name matches the builder or company you are contracting with.
3. Check the cover limits and expiry dates.
4. Confirm the type of cover, not just the existence of a policy.
5. If in doubt, contact the insurer or broker to confirm the policy is valid.

A genuine builder will not be offended by these checks. If they are reluctant, treat it as a warning sign.

## Qualifications to look for

Insurance protects you financially. Qualifications protect you technically. The right qualifications show that the builder has been trained and assessed.

### Trade-specific qualifications

- **Bricklayers** may hold NVQ Level 2 or 3 in Trowel Occupations.
- **Carpenters and joiners** may hold NVQ Level 2 or 3 in Wood Occupations.
- **Plasterers, roofers and painters** often hold relevant NVQs or apprenticeships.
- **Plumbers** may be qualified to NVQ Level 2 or 3 and registered with a competent person scheme.
- **Electricians** should be registered with a scheme such as [NICEIC](https://www.niceic.com/) or NAPIT.
- **Gas engineers** must be on the [Gas Safe Register](https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk/) by law.

Always ask for certificates and check registration numbers online where possible.

### CSCS cards

The Construction Skills Certification Scheme card shows that a worker has the appropriate training and qualifications for their job, and has passed a health and safety test. While not a legal requirement on all sites, it is a useful indicator of competence.

You can check CSCS cards on the official CSCS website. Be aware that some cards are labourer-only, so make sure the card matches the role the person will be doing.

### Trade body membership

Membership of a recognised trade body is a strong sign of credibility. Look for membership of:

- [Federation of Master Builders](https://www.fmb.org.uk/find-a-builder)
- [TrustMark](https://www.trustmark.org.uk/)
- [NICEIC](https://www.niceic.com/) for electrical work
- [Gas Safe Register](https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk/) for gas work
- [NHBC](https://www.nhbc.co.uk/) for new homes and warranties

Trade bodies often require members to meet standards, have insurance and agree to a code of conduct. Some also offer dispute resolution services if things go wrong.

## Checking company details

If you are dealing with a limited company, check the details on [Companies House](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/companies-house). Look at:

- How long the company has been trading.
- Whether accounts are up to date.
- Whether the company has been dissolved or struck off.
- The registered address and company officers.

A company with a short history or a history of dissolved companies may warrant extra caution.

## References and past work

Ask for references from recent jobs similar to yours. When you contact referees, ask:

- Did the builder finish on time?
- Did the price stay close to the quote?
- Was the site left tidy?
- Were there any defects and how were they handled?
- Would they use the builder again?

If possible, visit a completed project. Seeing the quality of finish with your own eyes is one of the best checks you can do.

## Online reviews and complaints

Reviews can be helpful, but they should not be the only check. Look at a range of sources and read the detail, not just the star rating. A few bad reviews among many good ones may be normal. A pattern of complaints about the same issue is more concerning.

If you want extra confidence, use review platforms such as [Checkatrade](https://www.checkatrade.com/) or [Which? Trusted Traders](https://trustedtraders.which.co.uk/). These sites verify traders and publish detailed feedback.

## Red flags that should make you walk away

- Refusal to provide proof of insurance.
- No qualifications, CSCS cards or trade body membership.
- Pressure to sign quickly or pay a large deposit in cash.
- No written quote or contract.
- Quotes that are far cheaper than others without explanation.
- No fixed address or use of only a mobile number.
- Poor communication or evasive answers.

Our guide on [how to spot a cowboy builder](/blog/how-to-spot-a-cowboy-builder) covers these warning signs in more detail. If you have already hired someone and suspect fraud, report it to [Action Fraud](https://www.actionfraud.police.uk/).

## Keep records

Once you have checked insurance and qualifications, keep copies of everything. Store:

- Insurance certificates.
- Qualification certificates.
- Trade body membership confirmations.
- Gas Safe and electrical registration numbers.
- References and correspondence.

These records will be useful if you need to make a claim, sell your home or prove compliance with building regulations.

## Final thoughts

Checking a builder's insurance and qualifications takes a little time, but it is one of the best investments you can make in your project. A professional builder will welcome your diligence. A cowboy builder will not.
