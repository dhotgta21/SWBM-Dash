## The brief

Haddenham Property Co. had a client in Didcot, Oxfordshire, who wanted a self-contained garden annex with an en-suite at the bottom of a long, narrow garden. The building was to be used as a home office with occasional guest accommodation, so it needed to be warm, secure and connected to mains services. The site was reached through a side gate that opened onto a grass path between two neighbouring fences.

We visited the property in late autumn, measured the gate width and the garden slope, and priced the full structural pack from the architect's sketch. Because the gate was too narrow for a pallet truck and the lawn would not support a loaded wheelbarrow after rain, we planned every drop as a hand-carry operation with protective boarding along the route.

The clients signed off the quote knowing the delivery method, the staged programme and the fact that the concrete base would require a small mixer rather than a ready-mix lorry. The programme was set at ten weeks, with the aim of completing the weather-tight shell before the Christmas cold set in.

The clients wanted the annex to last as long as the main house, so the base, insulation and vapour control were specified to residential rather than temporary standards. They were not looking for a summer room that would sag or leak after a few seasons; they wanted a durable, everyday space at the end of the garden. They also specified a small kitchenette and space for a sofa bed, which meant the electrical first-fix and plumbing had to be coordinated around our structural deliveries.

## The challenge

The Didcot garden had three main challenges. The first was access. The side gate was only 850mm wide and opened inward against a fence, so long timber lengths and full plasterboard sheets had to be carried through at an angle by two people. We therefore limited the length of C24 timber to 4.8m rather than the 6m sections the carpenter might normally have preferred.

The second challenge was the ground conditions. The garden sloped gently away from the house and held water after heavy rain. The concrete base had to be raised slightly above the surrounding lawn and include a damp-proof membrane to stop moisture being drawn up into the timber frame. The 22 tonnes of MOT Type 1 were used partly to create a stable, draining sub-base beneath the slab.

The third challenge was the en-suite. Because the annex included a shower room, the floor build-up had to allow for drainage falls and the walls had to be robust enough to support concealed pipework. We supplied extra hardwood plywood for the wet-area linings and made sure the plasterboard quantities included the ceiling as well as the walls.

The clients were both working from home, so noise and disruption had to be kept to weekday working hours. We booked deliveries for mid-morning, after the morning commute and before the garden was needed for lunch. The route was swept and the boarding lifted at the end of each working day. Because the garden was long and narrow, the clients could see the entire work area from the house, so site tidiness was particularly important.

Durability was built into the specification. On the Didcot garden building, the concrete base included a DPM, the timber frame was treated, and the insulation was protected by a vapour control layer. These details mean the Didcot building should outlast any lightweight shed and add lasting value to the property.

## Materials we supplied

Haddenham Property Co. sent us the specification and we priced the full structural pack in one quote, then split it into six small drops to suit the Didcot garden. The quantities we supplied were:

- **Foundations and sub-base:** 22 tonnes of **MOT Type 1 Aggregate**, 155 bags of **General Purpose Cement**, 14 tonnes of **Building Sand**, 14 tonnes of **20mm Ballast Aggregate**.
- **Timber and sheet materials:** 0.9 cubic m of **C24 Timber**, 293 linear m of **BS Treated Battens**, 7 sheets of **OSB3 Board 18mm**, 14 sheets of **Premium Hardwood Plywood**, 10 sheets of **Standard Plasterboards**, 16 sheets of **PIR Insulation Board**.
- **Fixings and hardware:** 4 boxes of **Galvanized Nails**, 7 boxes of **High Performance Wood Screws**.

The hardwood plywood was specified for the en-suite walls and any other areas where moisture resistance mattered. The OSB3 was used for the floor deck and any exposed structural sheathing. The PIR boards went into the walls and roof to keep the space warm with minimal heating.

All timber and sheet materials were stored flat at our yard until the day of dispatch to prevent warping or damage. The cement was palletised and shrink-wrapped so it could be lifted over the gate without the bags splitting. We also made sure the PIR boards were transported on edge only when fully supported, because the corners can be crushed if they are handled carelessly on a narrow path.

## Delivery and logistics

We structured the Didcot deliveries around hand-carry access and a compact working area. The first drop brought the MOT Type 1, cement, sand and ballast for the base, tipped on the driveway and barrowed through in small loads. The second drop brought the C24 timber and battens, carried through in pairs. The third and fourth drops brought the sheet materials, including plywood, OSB and plasterboard, on dry days only. The fifth drop was PIR insulation, and the sixth was fixings and a small top-up of cement.

Protective boarding was laid from the gate to the base and lifted each evening. The boards were old scaffold boards rather than plywood sheets, which gave enough protection without being so smooth that they became slippery in dew or light rain.

Because the quantities were small, several drops were combined onto a single 3.5-tonne tipper and then carried through by hand. The driver helped lift the heavier items over the gate and stacked them on the base slab so the carpenters could start work immediately.

The site manager called our trade counter the evening before each Didcot drop to confirm the timing and to warn us if rain had made the garden too soft. On one occasion a forecast of heavy rain led us to delay a plasterboard drop by 24 hours, holding the boards at our yard rather than risking them absorbing moisture in the garden.

A small reserve of cement and screws was kept at the yard against Haddenham Property Co.'s account. When the carpenter needed an extra box of screws after the frame proved more complex than the drawings suggested, it was delivered the same day on a vehicle already in the Didcot area. The reserve also covered an additional half-tonne of ballast that was needed when the groundworker found a soft pocket of clay that had to be dug out and replaced with compacted stone.

## The build

The garden annex in Didcot ran for ten weeks from the first excavation to handover. The groundworker stripped the topsoil and laid the MOT Type 1 sub-base before pouring the reinforced concrete slab. Once the slab had cured, the timber frame was built on top, with the floor joists raised on preservative-treated wall plates.

The C24 timber was used for the frame, the roof joists and the door and window studs. The battens formed the counter-battening for the cladding and the fixing grounds for the internal linings. The OSB3 deck went down before the insulation, and the PIR boards were cut to fit between the studs and packed tightly to avoid cold bridges. Because the en-suite generated moisture, the carpenter paid particular attention to sealing the vapour control layer before the plasterboard went on, and the hardwood plywood was used behind the shower area to give a robust backing for the tiles.

The en-suite was built at one end of the annex, with the hardwood plywood lining the shower enclosure and the plasterboard used on the remaining walls and ceiling. The plumber and electrician, supplied by other contractors, worked in sequence after the structural shell was weather-tight.

The site manager kept a simple programme board in the main house, marking off each stage as it was completed. When the carpenter finished the frame three days ahead of schedule, we moved the insulation drop forward so the cladding contractor could start on time.

Building control visited once during the build to check the structural elements and insulation. The site manager produced our delivery notes and the insulation certificates, which had been kept on file from the day the PIR boards arrived. The inspector noted that the documentation was well organised and that the insulation had been protected from moisture, both of which are common failings on garden building projects.

## The result

The completed annex is a warm, dry, year-round building at the end of the Didcot garden. It contains a main room large enough for a desk and sofa bed, a compact en-suite shower room, and a small kitchenette area. The clients use it as a home office during the week and as guest accommodation at weekends.

Haddenham Property Co. handed over on the agreed date in late January. The grass recovered quickly once the protective boarding was removed, and the neighbours commented that the build had caused less disturbance than they had expected. The clients have since used the annex through two winters and report that it stays warm with only a small electric heater, confirming that the insulation and vapour detailing were worth the careful installation.
